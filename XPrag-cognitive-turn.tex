\section{Experimental pragmatics and computational modeling}

The linguistic subfield of pragmatics studies how language is used for efficient communication and how communicated meaning is modulated, if not created, in a particular context by systematic, context-general patterns of use.
Its origins lie in the philosophy of language, in particular the analytical tradition of philosophy, in work on topics like \emph{speech-acts} \citep{Austin1962:How-to-do-Thing,Searle1969:Speech-Acts:-An}, \emph{conversational implicature} \citep{Grice1975:Logic-and-Conve}, or \emph{presupposition} \citep{Stalnaker1973:Presupposition,Karttunen1973:Presuppositions}.
Much early work on these topics remained invested in a logical-formal approach \citep{Gazdar1979:Pragmatics:-Imp,Kadmon2001:Formal-Pragmati}, but the early 2000's brought a deep and empowering incision: a widely adopted and ultimately very successful \emph{experimental turn} \citep{NoveckSperber2004:Experimental-Pr,Noveck2018:Experimental-Pr}, in parallel to similar empirical re-orientations in other hitherto very analytically minded subfields of linguistics like formal syntax \citep{Sprouse2007:A-program-for-e,Sprouse2023:The-Oxf-Handb-Exp-Syntax} and formal semantics \citep{MeibauerSteinbach2011:Experimental-Pr,CumminsKatsos2019:The-Oxford-Hand}.

The experimental turn in pragmatics was accompanied by formal and computational work from the start, with two main strands of approaches emerging at opposite ends of a spectrum.
On one end of the spectrum, \textit{grammaticalism} focuses on language as an abstract, computationally encapsulated system \mf{refs}.
Grammaticalism strives to give formally precise explanations for how pragmatic inferences (focusing almost exclusively on interpretation) could arise in a language-specific computational module \mf{refs}.
On the other end of the spectrum, \textit{probabilistic pragmatics} seeks explanations of pragmatic phenomena from the point of view that linguistic communication as a special form of social interaction, also taking aspects of domain-general cognition of language users into account \mf{refs}.
Figure~\ref{fig:genealogy-probprag} gives a coarse-grained overview of the development of, in particular, probabilistic pragmatics as the culmination point of research that combined aspects of individual cognition, such as bounded rationality \mf{refs}, and functional explanations based on optimal information transfer in communication \mf{refs}.

While experimental data can mitigate between alternative explanations proposed in the grammaticalist tradition \mf{refs}, I argue here that the more socio-cognitive approach adopted by probabilistic pragmatics is, if done right, much better suited to incorporate \textit{both} linguistic theory \textit{and} aspects of general cognition (such as perception, adaptation and decision-making), as well as the concrete affordances of a conversational situation or an experimental task, into a unified computational model of the \emph{data-generating process}, i.e., the (cognitive) processes by which individual data observations, such as concrete data points recorded from an experiment, come about.
Seen in this light, computational modeling in pragmatics can serve a bridging function between language, linguistic cognition and domain-general aspects of information-processing and decision-making.
The central argument of this position paper is, consequently, that a possible and highly productive future of experimental pragmatics in general consists in a dedicated \emph{cognitive turn}, which seeks to explain language use as the intricate interplay between language-specific and domain-general cognition.
Computational cognitive-pragmatic models plays a pivotal role in this cognitive turn, as they require detailed commitment about the role of linguistic theory (latent, established concepts) in the data-generating process, and the way that general cognitive factors shape linguistic behavior.
As I will argue here, recent work in modelling-oriented experimental pragmatics has started to ``go cognitive'' in this sense, but that a more dedicated embrace of the cognitive turn is needed.
Looking forward, I sketch possible future directions.

\section{Probabilistic pragmatics}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Notable early probabilistic approaches to pragmatics include pioneering work by \citet{Merin1994:Algebra-of-Elem,MerinInfoRelSocialDecisionmaking1999}, \citet{Zeevat2014:Language-Produc}, and \citet{Russell2012:Probabilistic-R}

  \item particularly prominent style of probabilistic modeling in pragmatics is the Rational Speech Act framework \citep{FrankGoodman2012:Predicting-Prag,ScontrasTessler2021:A-practical-int,Degen2023:The-Rational-Sp}, the basic set-up of which has been anticipated in decision-theoretic work by Anton Benz \citep{BenzUtil&RelAns2005,BenzvanRooijOptimalAssertions2007}

  \item what unites probabilistic pragmatics approaches is, arguably, the idea that pragmatic interpretation is a form of inverse reasoning, or abductive reasoning \citet{HobbsStickel1993:Interpretation-,BuntBlack2000:Abduction-Belie,FrankeJager2015:Probabilistic-p}: since it is natural to assume that the speaker's linguistic behavior (their choice of words, that they spoke in the first place, their intonation or speech-accompanying gesture, etc.) utterance is at least partly defined by a latent, i.e., unobservable, mental state of the speaker, the main task of pragmatic interpretation is to reason backwards, as it were, about how relatively likely or unlikely different conceivable causes for the speaker's behavior are

  \item This type of reasoning is modelled by Bayesian probabilistic reasoning: based on a probabilistic speaker policy $\pi(a \mid t)$ that determines how likely that speaker is to execute an action $a \in A$ if they are in state $t \in T$, and a prior distribution about how likely different states $t$ are, the normatively correct way of updating these prior beliefs is given by Bayes rule as follows:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Bayes-rule-interpretation}
   P(t \mid a) = \frac{\pi(a \mid t) \ P(t)}{\sum_{t' \in T} \pi(a \mid t') \ P(t')}
  \end{equation}

  \item to fill this basic (and arguably natural (at least as a starting point)) assumption of interpretation with life, at least three things have to specified:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item the relevant set of states $T$,
    \item the relevant set of actions $A$, and
    \item the speaker's behavioral policy $\pi$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item For a standard case of referential communication, for instance, the states $T$ could be potential referents in the shared perceptual context between speaker and listener, and the actions $A$ could be a finite set of utterances or descriptions, e.g., identified at the level of surface form, such as ``the blue triangle.''
  The speaker's policy $\pi$ could then be defined based on standard assumptions about how speakers are expected to behave in situations of the relevant kind, e.g., as envisaged by Grice's Maxims of Conversation \citep{Grice1975:Logic-and-Conve}.
  In particular, for a simple case of referential communication, we might define a probabilistic policy in terms of a utility function that score each action (utterance) $a$ in a given state $t$ based on $a$'s truth-value in $t$ (quality), the degree to which $a$ conveys information about $t$ in distinction to any other $t' \in T$, and the cost $C(a)$ of performing $a$:
  \begin{align*}
    \text{U}(a, t) = \text{Informativity}(a) + \text{Truth}(a,t) + \text{Cost}(a)
  \end{align*}
  The speaker's policy is then defined as a, possibly approximate, optimization of this utility function, e.g., as implemented by the softmax operation \citep{FrankeDegen2023:The-softmax-fun}:
  \begin{align*}
    \pi(a \mid t) = \frac{\expo \alpha \ U(a, t)}{\sum_{a' \in A} \expo \alpha \ U(a', t)}
  \end{align*}

  \item The standard formulation of the RSA-speaker policy, as given by \citet{FrankGoodman2012:Predicting-Prag} and much subsequent work, adheres to this general constraint-optimization picture, but is usually given in terms of a speaker optimizing their linguistic expressions for a literal listener. But as the literal listener is usually constructed as a context-blind, unstrategic dummy, we should more generally consider a probabilistic pragmatic model to consist of:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item a speaker policy that seeks to approximate optimal behavior under some (numeric) utility defining ``efficient / successful communication;'' and
    \item a Bayesian listener who inverts the speaker policy to infer likely explanations of the observed linguistic behavior.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \mf{maybe include:} higher-order iteration; multiple unknowns; more factors in the speaker utility function

\end{itemize}

\section{Theory-driven probabilistic modeling}
\label{sec:theory-driv-prob}

\begin{itemize}
  \item modeling in general:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item all models are wrong; some are useful
    \item even if all models are wrong, we should prefer models that are less wrong in the sense of Isaak
    \item so how our (less) wrong models useful?
    \item while for data: garbage in — garbage out
    \item for models: more assumptions in — more conclusions out
    \begin{itemize}
      \item e.g., causal modeling
      \item reasonable assumptions in; interesting conclusions out
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item all models contain some assumptions about the \textbf{data-generating process}
    \item more assumptions about the nature of that process → more information gained from the data
  \end{itemize}

\end{itemize}
